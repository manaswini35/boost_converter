Requirement 1 — "Use a mathematical approach, derive it"

This section derives the small-signal control-to-output transfer function from first principles using state-space averaging (the method used throughout Chapters 7–8 of Erickson & Maksimović, Fundamentals of Power Electronics), rather than quoting a formula. This directly explains why the original Kp = 0.7, Ki = 100 gains produce poor, non-smooth, high steady-state-error behavior.

1.1 Circuit equations for each switch state

Let $i_L(t)$ be the inductor current and $v(t)$ be the capacitor (output) voltage — the two state variables.

Switch ON (MOSFET conducting, $0 < t < dT_s$)

The inductor is connected directly across the input; the diode is reverse-biased so the output stage is isolated and the capacitor alone supplies the load. $$L\frac{di_L}{dt} = v_g(t) \qquad C\frac{dv}{dt} = -\frac{v}{R}$$

Switch OFF (MOSFET off, diode conducting, $dT_s < t < T_s$)

The inductor current now flows through the diode into the output. $$L\frac{di_L}{dt} = v_g(t) - v(t) \qquad C\frac{dv}{dt} = i_L(t) - \frac{v}{R}$$

1.2 Averaging over one switching period

Weighting each state's equation by the fraction of the period it's active ($d$ for ON, $d' = 1-d$ for OFF) gives the averaged (large-signal) model:

$$L\frac{d\langle i_L\rangle}{dt} = \langle v_g\rangle - d'(t)\,\langle v\rangle$$ $$C\frac{d\langle v\rangle}{dt} = d'(t)\,\langle i_L\rangle - \frac{\langle v\rangle}{R}$$

1.3 Steady-state (DC) operating point

Setting the derivatives to zero at the nominal duty ratio $D$:

$$0 = V_g - D'V \;\Rightarrow\; V = \frac{V_g}{D'}$$ $$0 = D'I_L - \frac{V}{R} \;\Rightarrow\; I_L = \frac{V}{D'R} = \frac{V_g}{D'^2R}$$
With $V_g = 13.4$ V and $D' = 0.67$: $\quad V = 20.0$ V   ✓ (matches reference)    $I_L = 7.46$ A

1.4 Perturbation and linearization

Introduce small AC perturbations on top of the DC values:

$$i_L = I_L+\hat{i}_L \quad v = V+\hat{v} \quad v_g = V_g+\hat{v}_g \quad d = D+\hat{d} \quad d' = D'-\hat{d}$$

Substituting into the averaged equations and expanding the product terms, e.g.:

$$d'(t)\,v(t) = (D'-\hat d)(V+\hat v) = D'V + D'\hat v - V\hat d - \hat d\hat v$$

The term $\hat d \hat v$ is a product of two small AC quantities — by the small-signal approximation this is dropped as negligibly small. Doing the same for $d'(t)\,i_L(t)$ and discarding the DC (steady-state) terms leaves the following linear AC equations:

$$L\frac{d\hat{i}_L}{dt} = \hat{v}_g - D'\hat{v} + V\hat{d} \tag{A}$$ $$C\frac{d\hat{v}}{dt} = D'\hat{i}_L - I_L\hat{d} - \frac{\hat{v}}{R} \tag{B}$$

1.5 Laplace transform and solving for $G_{vd}(s)$

To isolate the control-to-output transfer function $G_{vd}(s) = \hat v(s)/\hat d(s)$, set the line disturbance $\hat v_g(s) = 0$ and Laplace-transform (A) and (B):

$$sL\hat{I}_L = -D'\hat{V} + V\hat{D} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \hat{I}_L = \frac{V\hat{D}-D'\hat{V}}{sL}$$ $$sC\hat{V} = D'\hat{I}_L - I_L\hat{D} - \frac{\hat{V}}{R}$$

Substituting the first result into the second and collecting all $\hat V$ terms on the left:

$$\hat{V}\left[sC+\frac{D'^2}{sL}+\frac{1}{R}\right] = \hat{D}\left[\frac{D'V}{sL}-I_L\right]$$

Multiplying through by $sL$, and using $I_L = V/(D'R)$ to simplify the bracket on the right:

$$\frac{D'V}{sL}\cdot sL - I_L\cdot sL = D'V - \frac{sLV}{D'R} = D'V\left[1-\frac{sL}{D'^2R}\right]$$

Dividing both sides by $D'^2$ and rearranging into standard normalized form gives the final result:

$$G_{vd}(s) = \frac{\hat v(s)}{\hat d(s)}\bigg|_{\hat v_g=0} = \frac{V}{D'}\cdot\frac{1-\dfrac{sL}{D'^2R}}{1+\dfrac{sL}{D'^2R}+\dfrac{s^2LC}{D'^2}}$$

This can be written in the standard salient-features form:

$$G_{vd}(s) = G_{d0}\cdot\frac{1-\dfrac{s}{\omega_z}}{1+\dfrac{s}{Q\omega_0}+\left(\dfrac{s}{\omega_0}\right)^2}$$

with the following identifications (matching each coefficient):

FeatureFormulaMeaning
$G_{d0}$$V/D'$DC gain (duty-to-voltage)
$\omega_0$$D'/\sqrt{LC}$Resonant double-pole frequency (LC filter)
$Q$$D'R\sqrt{C/L}$Quality factor of the resonance
$\omega_z$$D'^2R/L$Right-half-plane (RHP) zero

1.6 Numerical evaluation for this converter

$$G_{d0} = \frac{20}{0.67} = 29.85 \text{ V}$$ $$\omega_0 = \frac{0.67}{\sqrt{(76.8\text{µH})(400\text{µF})}} = 3823 \text{ rad/s} \;\Rightarrow\; f_0 = 608 \text{ Hz}$$ $$Q = (0.67)(4)\sqrt{\frac{400\text{µF}}{76.8\text{µH}}} = 6.1$$ $$\omega_z = \frac{(0.67)^2(4)}{76.8\text{µH}} = 23{,}385\text{ rad/s} \;\Rightarrow\; f_z = 3.72\text{ kHz}$$
Why this explains your professor's comment. The double pole at $f_0=608$ Hz has $Q=6.1$ — fairly peaky. Evaluating the phase of $G_{vd}(j\omega)$ shows the plant contributes about $-170^\circ$ of phase lag by 750 Hz, and crosses past $-180^\circ$ around 1 kHz. With the old gains ($K_p=0.7$), the loop gain at high frequency is $\approx G_{d0}\cdot K_p \approx 21$ — far above unity — which forces the actual loop crossover out into this already-unstable phase region. That is precisely why the current/output waveform looked noisy with persistent error instead of settling cleanly: the loop was operating close to marginal stability, not truly regulating.

Requirement 2 — "Use a PID method so you can show you derived and learned it"

This section designs new PID gains analytically using classical frequency-response (phase margin) methods, rather than trial-and-error tuning.

2.1 Selecting a safe crossover frequency

Two hard constraints from the plant, established in Section 1:

  • Crossover must stay well below $f_0 = 608$ Hz — above this, phase collapses toward $-180^\circ$ due to the resonant double pole.
  • Crossover must stay well below $f_z = 3.72$ kHz — the RHP zero adds further lag on top of the poles.

Choosing $f_c = 500$ Hz ($\omega_c = 3142$ rad/s) satisfies both with margin.

2.2 Evaluating the plant at crossover

$$\frac{s}{\omega_0}\bigg|_{\omega_c} = \frac{3142}{3823}=0.822 \qquad \frac{s}{\omega_z}\bigg|_{\omega_c}=\frac{3142}{23385}=0.134$$ Denominator: $\; 1-(0.822)^2 + j\dfrac{0.822}{Q} = 0.324+j0.134 \Rightarrow |{\cdot}|=0.351,\;\angle = 22.5^\circ$ Numerator: $\;1-j(0.134) \Rightarrow |{\cdot}| = 1.009,\;\angle=-7.65^\circ$ $$|G_{vd}(j\omega_c)| = 29.85\times\frac{1.009}{0.351} = 85.9 \qquad \angle G_{vd}(j\omega_c) = -7.65^\circ-22.5^\circ=-30.2^\circ$$

2.3 Solving for compensator gain and phase

The PWM comparator contributes modulator gain $F_m = 1/V_{pp} = 1$ (sawtooth spans 0 → 1). For unity loop gain at crossover:

$$|G_c(j\omega_c)|\cdot|G_{vd}(j\omega_c)| = 1 \;\Rightarrow\; |G_c(j\omega_c)| = \frac{1}{85.9} = 0.01164$$

Targeting a phase margin of $PM = 60^\circ$: total loop phase at crossover must equal $-(180^\circ-PM)=-120^\circ$. Since the plant already contributes $-30.2^\circ$, the compensator itself must not add more than about $-90^\circ$ (the natural maximum for a PI term) — placing the integrator zero well below crossover keeps this well within bounds, so the design is comfortably achievable.

2.4 PID structure and gain solution

Using $G_c(s) = K_p + \dfrac{K_i}{s} + K_ds$, with the integrator zero $\omega_{zc}=K_i/K_p$ placed at $2\pi(50\text{ Hz}) = 314$ rad/s (a decade below crossover, standard practice):

$$K_p \approx \frac{0.01164}{\sqrt{1+(314/3142)^2}} \approx 0.0116$$ $$K_i = K_p\cdot\omega_{zc} = 0.0116\times 314 \approx 3.6$$ $$K_d \approx 3\times10^{-6}\;\text{(small derivative term, adds phase lead near }f_0\text{ to damp the }Q=6.1\text{ resonance)}$$
Resulting phase margin ≈ 144° — a well-damped, non-oscillatory design with fast settling and zero DC steady-state error (guaranteed by the integrator term).

Requirement 3 — Controller Transfer Function, Poles/Zeros & Final Tuning

3.1 Continuous-time controller transfer function

$$G_c(s) = K_p + \frac{K_i}{s} + K_ds = 0.0116 + \frac{3.6}{s}+3\times10^{-6}s$$

Written as a single rational function (common denominator $s$):

$$G_c(s) = \frac{K_ds^2+K_ps+K_i}{s} = \frac{3\times10^{-6}s^2+0.0116s+3.6}{s}$$

3.2 Poles and zeros of the controller

The controller has one pole at the origin (the integrator, $s=0$) and two zeros found from the quadratic numerator $K_ds^2+K_ps+K_i=0$:

$$s = \frac{-K_p\pm\sqrt{K_p^2-4K_dK_i}}{2K_d}$$
$$s = \frac{-0.0116\pm\sqrt{(0.0116)^2-4(3\times10^{-6})(3.6)}}{2(3\times10^{-6})}$$ $$= \frac{-0.0116\pm\sqrt{1.346\times10^{-4}-4.32\times10^{-5}}}{6\times10^{-6}} = \frac{-0.0116\pm0.00956}{6\times10^{-6}}$$
$$z_1 \approx -343\text{ rad/s}\;(f\approx 54.6\text{ Hz}) \qquad z_2 \approx -3527\text{ rad/s}\;(f\approx 561\text{ Hz})$$ Controller pole: $\;s=0$ (integrator)

This is a Type-III-like structure: the two zeros sit near the plant's resonant frequency region (561 Hz is close to $f_0=608$ Hz), giving the phase boost needed right where the LC double pole would otherwise collapse the phase — exactly the intended cancellation strategy referenced in your design-method PDF for boost-type converters.

3.4 Discrete-time conversion (for Simulink's Discrete PID block)

With sample time $T_s = 40$ µs (one update per switching period) and the Tustin (trapezoidal) transform $s \to \dfrac{2}{T_s}\cdot\dfrac{z-1}{z+1}$, Simulink's Discrete PID Controller block performs this conversion internally once you enter the continuous-equivalent gains and select the Trapezoidal integrator method — no manual z-domain algebra is required. The resulting discrete compensator has the form:

$$G_c(z) = K_p + K_i\cdot T_s\cdot\frac{z+1}{2(z-1)} + K_d\cdot\frac{2}{T_s}\cdot\frac{z-1}{z+1}$$

Simulink Implementation Summary

  1. Open the PID Controller block dialog (already discrete-time).
  2. Change Controller dropdown from "PI" to "PID".
  3. Set P = 0.0116, I = 3.6, D = 3e-6, N = 100.
  4. Set Sample time = 4e-5.
  5. Expand "Integrator and Filter methods" → set integrator method to Trapezoidal.
  6. Go to the Saturation tab → confirm Limit output is checked, Lower = 0.1, Upper = 0.9.
  7. Click Apply/OK, save, and re-run the simulation.
  8. Compare the new Scope3 trace against the old one: output should settle at 20 V with visibly reduced ripple/noise on the current waveform, and no lingering offset after each input-voltage step.

Conclusion

The original PI gains ($K_p=0.7,\,K_i=100$) were selected without reference to the converter's actual small-signal dynamics, driving the loop crossover into a frequency region where the LC resonance and RHP zero had already collapsed the phase past $-180^\circ$ — producing the noisy, non-settling behavior your professor flagged. By deriving $G_{vd}(s)$ from first principles (state-space averaging), identifying the plant's poles ($f_0=608$ Hz, Q=6.1) and RHP zero ($f_z=3.72$ kHz), and designing a PID compensator analytically around a safe crossover frequency (500 Hz, 144° phase margin), the new gains ($K_p=0.0116,\,K_i=3.6,\,K_d=3\times10^{-6}$) should deliver a smooth, well-damped step response with zero steady-state error.
